Realms

The states, dynasties, and empires that rose and fell across this timeline — from the Sabirid Dynasty in the Levant to the republics of a very different Europe.

Timeline Figures Events Realms
Founded 1178 · Levant

Sabirid Dynasty

The Sabirid dynasty, also known as the House of Sabira or the Jasmine Dynasty, was the ruling house of the state later known as the Imperial Throne of Jasmine, commonly referred to as the Jasmine Throne. Founded by Sabira bint Yusef following the collapse of the Kingdom of Jerusalem in 1178, the dynasty governed much of the Levant and neighboring regions for over six centuries before its gradual dissolution during the modern era.

The Sabirids emerged during the political fragmentation that followed the decline of Crusader authority in the eastern Mediterranean. Over the course of the late 12th and 13th centuries, the dynasty transformed Jerusalem from the unstable capital of a collapsing Crusader kingdom into the administrative center of a durable regional empire whose influence extended across Palestine, southern Syria, Egypt, and parts of Mesopotamia.

The dynasty is chiefly remembered for its administrative centralization, restoration of long-distance trade networks, and sponsorship of a cultural and intellectual flourishing later termed the Jasmine Enlightenment by imperial historians. Later Sabirid political thought also associated the dynasty with the doctrine known as The Sovereign’s Trust, which framed imperial legitimacy around the ruler’s responsibility to maintain justice, prosperity, and political stability.

The dynasty also played a central role in the development of Sabirism, a religious and philosophical tradition centered on the reverence of Sabira as a divinely favored restorer of order and harmony. Over time, Sabirism became deeply intertwined with imperial identity and court ceremonial culture.

Although later imperial chroniclers portrayed the Sabirid period as an uninterrupted golden age, modern historians generally describe the dynasty’s history as one of cycles of consolidation, regional fragmentation, reform, and recovery shaped by shifting trade patterns, military competition, and internal factionalism.

1178 – 1956 · Middle East & N. Africa

Jasmine Empire

The Jasmine Empire, formally known as The Imperial Throne of Jasmine, and also referred to historically as the Sabirid Empire, was a major world empire that dominated the Middle East, North Africa, and the Eastern Mediterranean from its foundation in 1178 until its gradual decline and transformation in the modern era. Forged in the Jasmine Revolt led by Sabira bint Yusef, the empire represented a radical break from the preceding feudal and clerical orders, establishing a centralized, bureaucratic state guided by the secular-imperial philosophy of The Sovereign’s Trust.

The empire is renowned for its prolonged period of internal stability, the Pax Sabirica, which fostered the Jasmine Enlightenment, a cultural and scientific renaissance that produced significant advancements in law, architecture, astronomy, and literature. Its capital, Jerusalem, was transformed from a contested holy city into the administrative and intellectual heart of a vast, multi-ethnic polity.

1707 – mid-20th c. · Iberian Peninsula

Empire of Iberia

The Empire of Iberia (Spanish: Imperio de Iberia, Portuguese: Império Ibérico) was a dual monarchy that ruled Spain and Portugal and their overseas colonies from 1707 until its collapse in the mid-20th century. At its height, Iberia was one of the most influential powers in Europe, Africa, and Asia, though often overshadowed by its rivals Gaul and the Britannic Commonwealth.

Founded 1799 · France

Empire of Gaul

The Empire of Gaul (French: Empire de Gaule) was a European empire established in 1799, following the collapse of the Bourbon monarchy and the rise of a new republican-imperial system. Styling itself as heir to Rome and Charlemagne, Gaul became one of the most industrially advanced and culturally influential states of the 19th century.

Founded 1820 · Britain

Britannic Commonwealth

The Britannic Commonwealth was a republican state founded in 1820 after the collapse of the English monarchy during the Industrial Riots. It retained much of Britain’s industrial empire and emerged as a dominant global naval power through the 19th century.

Founded 1857 · Scandinavia

Norse Union of Odin

The Norse Union of Odin (Old Norse: Óðins Samfylking Norðurlanda) was a pan-Scandinavian monarchy established in 1857 following the Norse National Awakening. It unified Denmark, Norway, and Sweden under a neo-pagan monarchy that revived Norse traditions and adopted Odin as a symbolic patron.

Founded 1649 · Ireland

Kingdom of Ireland

The Kingdom of Ireland (Irish: Ríogacht na hÉireann) was a sovereign monarchy established in 1649 after the successful expulsion of English forces during the Gaelic Wars of Liberation. By the 19th century, Ireland had become a major naval and colonial power, rivaling the Britannic Commonwealth.

1636 – 17th c. collapse · China

Qing Dynasty

The Qing dynasty (Chinese: 清朝; Qīng Cháo), officially the Great Qing, was a Manchu-led imperial dynasty that ruled China from 1636 until the effective collapse of centralized authority during the Tianming Rebellion in 1673. Founded by the Aisin Gioro clan in Manchuria, the dynasty emerged from the political fragmentation and military crises that marked the final decades of the Ming dynasty. Although the Qing succeeded in conquering much of China and establishing themselves as the dominant power in East Asia, their rule remained comparatively short-lived in this timeline, lasting approximately forty-eight years as a unified empire before widespread rebellion and regional fragmentation shattered imperial authority.

The Qing period was characterized by rapid military expansion, attempts at administrative consolidation, and growing integration into a Eurasian trade network increasingly influenced by the Sabirid Empire and the wider Sabirist world. Historians often describe the dynasty as a transitional state caught between traditional imperial structures and an emerging age of transcontinental ideological exchange. The dynasty’s inability to adapt to mounting economic pressures, social unrest, and the spread of new political and philosophical currents ultimately contributed to its destabilization and the outbreak of the Tianming Rebellion in 1673.

1649 – early 21st c. · China

Fu Dynasty

The Fu dynasty (Chinese: 傅朝; pinyin: Fù Cháo), retrospectively known as the Later Fu by modern historians, was the ruling dynasty of China from 1649 until its dissolution in the early 21st century. Emerging from the collapse of the Qing dynasty during the Wars of Heavenly Order, the Fu state unified much of China under a centralized imperial system that blended traditional Chinese bureaucracy with the universalist political philosophy spreading westward from the Jasmine world. Lasting for more than three and a half centuries, the dynasty became one of the longest-ruling imperial houses in Chinese history and profoundly shaped the political, cultural, and economic development of East Asia.

The dynasty was founded by Fu Chengyuan, a military governor and reformist commander who seized Beijing amid the fragmentation of Qing authority. Rejecting the hereditary aristocratic structures and rigid social hierarchy associated with the late Qing court, the Fu rulers promoted an ideology centered upon civic order, meritocratic governance, and moral stewardship by the state. Historians often describe the dynasty as a hybrid system: Chinese in administrative structure and cultural identity, yet deeply influenced by Sabirid concepts of centralized authority and rational statecraft transmitted across Inner Asia during the seventeenth century.

At its territorial height in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, the Fu dynasty governed most of China proper, Mongolia, Tibet, and parts of Central Asia through tributary protectorates and military governorships. Its long period of internal stability and economic expansion became known as the Era of Harmonious Governance, during which China experienced rapid urban growth, commercial integration, infrastructural expansion, and a flourishing of literature, engineering, and philosophy. Despite repeated periods of reform and recovery, the dynasty gradually weakened during the twentieth century under the pressures of industrial modernization, regional nationalism, foreign economic penetration, and constitutional unrest. The imperial system formally ended in 2014 following the Beijing Settlement Accords and the dissolution of the Imperial Secretariat.

1889 – reorganized · Central Europe

Preußischer Volksbund

The Prussian People's Commonwealth (German: Preußischer Volksbund), commonly known as the People's Prussia, was a republican state that existed in Central Europe from 1889 until its reorganization into the German Federation in the mid-20th century. Established following the political upheavals that followed the First Great Asian War, the Commonwealth succeeded the Kingdom of Prussia and marked the end of more than two centuries of Hohenzollern rule.

The Commonwealth emerged during a period of economic crisis, social unrest, and political disillusionment. Although Prussia had emerged from the First Great Asian War without military defeat, the enormous human and financial costs of the conflict undermined confidence in the monarchy and the traditional aristocratic order. The establishment of the Commonwealth represented one of the most significant political transformations in modern European history and became a model for several republican movements across the continent.

Sacred Babenberg Realm · Central Europe

Heretical Austrian State

The Sacred Babenberg Realm (Latin: Sacrum Regnum Foederis), commonly referred to by foreign powers as the Heretical Austrian State (Latin: Haereticus Civitas Austriae) was a Central European monarchy that existed from 1181 until its dissolution in the aftermath of the Second Great Asian War. Ruled by the House of Babenberg and centered on Vienna, the realm emerged during the collapse of the traditional Abrahamic religious order that followed the rise of Sabira bint Yusef and the establishment of the Sabirid state in the Levant.

The realm occupied a unique position in world history as the principal surviving state dedicated to preserving the doctrines, liturgy, and institutions of pre-1178 Christianity. While much of Europe gradually adopted new philosophical traditions, pagan revivals, or secular state ideologies, the Babenberg rulers maintained that they alone represented the legitimate continuation of the ancient covenant between humanity and the Divine. This conviction shaped the state's politics, culture, and foreign relations for more than seven centuries.

1956 – 2012 · Society & culture

Life in the Eternal Sabirid State

Life inside the Eternal Sabirid State (1956–2012) was marked by strict ideological control, ethnic exclusion, and pervasive propaganda. Citizens lived under the doctrine of Eternal Purity, which elevated the “Sabirid Arab” identity as the sole bearer of rights and privileges. While the regime claimed to safeguard stability after the Second Great Asian War, daily existence was characterized by surveillance, repression, and limited freedoms.
