Longer write-ups of the wars, revolts, and turning points mentioned on the Timeline.

1874 – 1882

First Great Asian War

The First Great Asian War (1874–1882), also known as the War of the Crescent and Dragon, was a global conflict fought between the Eastern Concord, principally the Jasmine Empire, the Fu Dynasty, and later the Mongol Confederation, and the Northern League, led by Prussia, the Norse Union, and the Kingdom of Ireland. The war was fought primarily across Central Asia, the Middle East, the Balkans, and the western Pacific, though its economic and political consequences were felt worldwide.

One of the deadliest conflicts of the 19th century, the war mobilized more than twenty million military personnel and caused an estimated eight to twelve million deaths, including civilians killed through famine, disease, and industrialized warfare. Historians commonly regard it as the first modern industrial war in Eurasia and the conflict that ended the Age of Inspiration while ushering in the Age of Steel.

The war radically altered the balance of power between Europe and Asia. Although no side achieved a decisive victory, the conflict accelerated industrialization in the eastern empires, strengthened Prussian influence in Europe, and destabilized the old imperial order across Eurasia. The unresolved tensions of the war later contributed directly to the outbreak of the Second Great Asian War in the early twentieth century.

1891 – 1893

The Balkan War of 1891

The Balkan War of 1891 (1891–1893) was a short but destructive conflict fought primarily between the Kingdom of Prussia and the Jasmine Empire in the Balkans. Though often overshadowed by the First Great Asian War (1874–1882), historians widely regard the Balkan War as the true death knell of Prussia and the beginning of the Jasmine Empire’s decline.

The war began with disputes over the status of Bulgaria and Serbia, territories heavily contested during the First Great Asian War. Both empires sought to expand influence in the unstable region, but exhausted populations and fragile economies made prolonged fighting unsustainable.

1893

Fall of Prussia

The Fall of Prussia refers to the collapse of the Kingdom of Prussia in 1893, following its defeat and exhaustion in the Balkan War of 1891–1893. Once regarded as the military and industrial core of the Iron Pact, Prussia’s downfall marked the end of its two-century role as a great power in Europe and left a vacuum that reshaped continental politics.

1890s

The Jasmine Empire's Revolts of the 1890s

The Revolts of the 1890s were a series of uprisings, strikes, and rebellions that shook the Jasmine Empire following its costly participation in the Balkan War (1891–1893). Though often compared to the Fall of Prussia (1893) in scale and intensity, the Jasmine Empire ultimately survived the crisis, owing to a combination of brutal suppression, tentative reform, and monumental advances in medicine that gave the empire a new sense of purpose.

1906 – 1932

Road to the Second Great Asian War

The Road to the Second Great Asian War (1906–1932) refers to the period of mounting global tensions that culminated in the outbreak of the Second Great Asian War in 1932. Historians widely interpret this era as the consequence of unresolved conflicts from the First Great Asian War and subsequent Balkan War, combined with the rise of aggressive nationalist states and the destabilization of older empires.

1932 – 1956

Second Great Asian War

The Second Great Asian War (1932–1956) was a global conflict fought between the Hilāl Bloc — the Jasmine Empire, Qing Dynasty, Mongol Empire, and Iberia — and the Iron Pact — the Volkstaat, Norse Union of Odin, Kingdom of Ireland, Empire of Gaul, and the Britannic Commonwealth. It was the deadliest conflict in recorded history, resulting in an estimated 94 million deaths and reshaping the political map of Eurasia, Africa, and the Indian Ocean world.

Late 17th century

Tianming Rebellion

The Tianming Rebellion (Chinese: 天命之乱; Tiānmìng zhī Luàn; lit. “Disorder of the Heavenly Mandate”) was a major anti-Qing uprising that contributed to the collapse of centralized Qing authority during the late 17th century. Beginning in 1673 in the lower Yangtze region, the rebellion emerged from a convergence of anti-Manchu sentiment, economic instability, social unrest, and the spread of new political and philosophical currents influenced in part by Sabirist thought transmitted through Indian Ocean and Central Asian trade networks.

What began as a loosely organized reform movement gradually evolved into a broad coalition of rural militias, urban secret societies, dissident scholars, merchants, laborers, and former Ming loyalists united by the belief that the Qing dynasty had lost the Mandate of Heaven. The rebellion ultimately devastated large parts of southern China, permanently weakened imperial authority, and accelerated the fragmentation of Qing rule during the late 17th century. Historians generally regard the conflict as one of the defining political crises of early modern East Asia.

Primary source · compiled c. 1563

The Chronicles of the Reign of the Mother

The most authoritative surviving Sabirid account of the fall of Baldwin IV and the rise of Sabira bint Yusef — six books, from the waning of Frankish Jerusalem through the Liberation War, the taking of the city in 1178, and the Mother’s legacy. Compiled by later historians of the Jasmine Throne around 1563.
